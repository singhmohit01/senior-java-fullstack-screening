1. Unconventional Route

One experience that describes an unconventional route I took was in a project where we had an existing implementation using OpenSearch and synchronous APIs to retrieve folder-size information. The existing solution was working, so the straightforward approach would have been to continue with it and make incremental optimizations. However, as the data volume and real-time requirements increased, I felt that simply optimizing the existing synchronous flow would not address the underlying performance and scalability challenges. I proposed moving to Elasticsearch and using Kafka with an asynchronous processing approach, even though it meant changing a working solution rather than just improving the existing one.

I worked on understanding the Elasticsearch components and how we could use them effectively for indexing and searching large volumes of data, while Kafka handled the asynchronous flow and helped decouple the services. The results were then exposed through asynchronous REST APIs and integrated into the React.js frontend. The redesigned approach reduced the average API response time by around 60% compared with the previous synchronous implementation, while the Elasticsearch-based microservices were able to support searching across more than 50 million records with sub-second search responses. **For me, the key learning was that an unconventional approach does not necessarily mean introducing something new just for the sake of it. It means being willing to challenge a working solution when the business requirements, scale, or performance expectations have changed, and then proving that the new approach provides a meaningful improvement.**

2. Intense Focus

One experience that stands out was when I took up the role of **SDET Lead for the Platform Solutions project for the first time**. I was responsible for building the automation coverage using Cucumber BDD and Playwright, with a target of creating more than 200 test cases within approximately one month. It was a challenging timeline, and instead of immediately starting to write test cases, I spent time understanding the application functionality, business workflows, user roles, and different scenarios around fraud records, bulk uploads, approvals, edits, and deletions. I wanted to make sure that I was not just completing a number of test cases, but building automation that would actually be useful and maintainable.

**I stayed focused on this task throughout the month and continuously analyzed the application to identify common patterns that could be reused. Based on that analysis, I designed generic Cucumber feature files, reusable step definitions, and Playwright components so that we could cover multiple scenarios without duplicating automation code.** There were several iterations where I had to rethink the approach and make the framework more generic while still meeting the deadline. We eventually completed more than 200 automated test cases within the required timeframe. This experience helped me grow significantly because it was my first experience leading SDET activities at that scale. It improved my technical understanding of automation architecture, but more importantly, it taught me how to stay focused under a tight deadline, prioritize what really matters, and balance speed with long-term maintainability.

3. Joining a New Team/Project

One experience that stands out was when I joined my last project, a **Content Repository Management System**. Although I had more than 8.8+ years of experience in software development, this project was quite different from the systems I had worked on previously [always worked on banking Domain]. The application had a complex user hierarchy and required us to perform different operations on users and content, with OpenSearch and Elasticsearch being important parts of the architecture. The deployment and infrastructure side was also new to me, especially working extensively with Docker and Kubernetes, including Kubernetes YAML configurations. The overall architecture was different from what I had experienced before, so there was a significant amount of technical and business context that I needed to understand within a short period of time.

I approached the learning process by first understanding the bigger picture instead of trying to learn every component individually. I started with the overall architecture, major business flows, service interactions, and data flow, and then gradually went deeper into the areas relevant to my work. I spent time going through the existing code and deployment configurations and also worked closely with the senior architects from the client side. Discussions with them were particularly valuable because they helped me understand the reasoning behind architectural decisions rather than just knowing how something was implemented. I also prioritized smaller development tasks and issues initially, which allowed me to learn the system through hands-on experience while contributing to the project. As my understanding improved, I gradually took ownership of more complex development and deployment-related activities. This experience taught me that when entering a complex environment, it is important to build a clear mental model first, prioritize the areas that directly impact your work, and then expand your knowledge through continuous hands-on learning.
